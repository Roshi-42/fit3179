Australia: A Nation of Frogs and Amphibians

From tropical rainforests to coastal wetlands and inland habitats, Australia's frogs can be find in various environments.

But frog diversity is not distributed equally across the country. Where do the greatest numbers of different species occur, and which states have recorded the widest variety of frogs?

01. Where Is Frog Diversity Greatest?

The Australian Frog Atlas maps how the number of different frog species varies geographically. Darker regions indicate higher species richness.

Species richness refers to the number of different species in certain areas, not the total number of frogs in an area.

Source: Australian Frog Atlas 4.0 (2026).

From Geographic Diversity to Recorded Species

The first map shows how frog species richness varies across Australia. However, another way to understand frog diversity is to examine which species have been recorded by people in different states and territories.

The FrogID citizen-science project provides observations submitted across Australia, allowing us to compare recorded frog diversity between states.

02. Which States Have the Most Recorded Frog Species?

This map compares the number of distinct frog species identified through FrogID observations in each Australian state and territory.

These counts represent species recorded through FrogID rather than the total number of frog species living in each state. Recording effort varies between regions.

Source: Australian Museum, FrogID Dataset 7.0 (2017–2024).

What Do the Observations Reveal?

Queensland has the highest recorded species richness in this FrogID dataset, with 106 distinct species. New South Wales follows with 84, while Western Australia has 77.

These differences may reflect environmental conditions, geographic distributions and variation in recording effort. They should not be interpreted as a direct measure of total frog populations.

08. Where Are Australia's Frog Recording Hotspots?

FrogID observations are not distributed evenly across Australia. This map shows the geographic concentration of frog occurrence records, with larger circles indicating more records within a one-degree geographic grid cell.

Each circle represents a 1° × 1° geographic cell. High recording activity may reflect proximity to population centres and differences in citizen-science participation, rather than greater frog population abundance.

Source: Australian Museum, FrogID Dataset 7.0.
